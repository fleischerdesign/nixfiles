# Creative Direction

## When to load & job

Use this for a campaign concept or a direction that writers, designers, and channel owners need to interpret together. Decide what the audience should take away and what idea carries it. Art direction gives that idea a visual treatment; this brief should survive a change in treatment.

Build the direction as a campaign prototype and a brief for the people making its parts, not a mood board or a forecast of audience response.

## Inputs & concepts

Get the audience and occasion, desired action, actual offer or story, known objections, and claims the owner can substantiate. Ask channel owners where people may enter the experience and which next steps they control; get approved names, tone, and brand relationships from the brand owner. Mark audience assumptions as hypotheses when evidence is absent. Keep unsupported claims out of the sample copy as well as the brief.

The **tension** is the friction between what the audience wants or believes and what the offer asks them to consider. The **proposition** is the one thing the audience should take away; the **reason to believe** is the supplied fact, demonstration or experience that supports it. Neither is necessarily a headline. A **creative device** is a repeatable way of expressing the idea, such as posing a repair question and answering it with a specific tool and borrowing step. **Channel jobs** describe what each encounter must do, including encounters that do not happen in sequence.

## Decisions & procedure

- When people understand the offer but doubt it, lead with a demonstrable truth: you give up some surprise for specificity. When people cannot imagine using an unfamiliar offer, lead with a recognizable situation: you give up some room to explain the mechanics.
- A repeated line carries the same thought through fragmented encounters but can waste the attention of a person who follows it to the next placement. A repeatable device gives each channel new work to do but depends more on coordination between owners.

1. Put the audience hesitation beside the action, then write two propositions that address it differently. For separating the actual choice from the requested campaign tactic, use [Option framing](knowledge/option-framing.md) (`references/knowledge/option-framing.md`). Give each route a sample opening and next action so the recommendation is more than a choice between slogans.
2. Make a **campaign spine**: one row per encounter and columns for what the person may already know, what this placement adds, the evidence it can carry, and the next action. Fill the same rows for both routes. Include an encounter that starts later in the sequence, such as a shared link or partner post.
3. Sketch the same placements with real offer details and short blocks of sample copy. Where the source of trust changes by channel, use [Identity systems](knowledge/identity-systems.md) (`references/knowledge/identity-systems.md`) to decide whose name or endorsement carries that role.
4. Recommend a route and name what it sacrifices. In the brief, give writers and channel owners the proposition, demonstration, device or line, and handoff between encounters. Separate details they can vary from facts or actions that have to remain consistent.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A tool library owner supplies a current inventory list and the actual borrowing steps. The unfamiliar-offer route opens with “What would you fix?” and names a crooked cupboard door; the poster invites the viewer to see what they can borrow. In the next row of the spine, the landing page must add more than the repair question: show the listed tool relevant to that job and put borrowing instructions beside it. Give that page a plain heading identifying the tool library so a visitor arriving from a shared link does not need to have seen the question. The credible-offer route instead opens on a specific tool and the borrowing process; it spends less time making repair imaginable but makes the service concrete sooner. Do not imply a tool is available today from an inventory list alone.

Repeating the launch headline everywhere preserves wording but makes a poster referral land on the same promise without instructions. Keep the connective idea and let the landing page advance it with owner-supplied details.

## Standards, limits & handover

For a campaign planning method, see Stephen King’s account of the planning cycle in *A Master Class in Brand Planning: The Timeless Works of Stephen King* (2007); `verify` the section in the edition in use. It is a planning model, not a norm. The campaign spine is a working convention; actual claim wording, brand rules and channel limits are project specifications supplied by their owners. Where U.S. advertising substantiation is relevant, consult the FTC's [Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation) (1984), on the reasonable basis for objective claims before dissemination; `verify` applicability with the relevant specialist rather than treating it as a global rule.

Show both concept routes in the same placements, the recommended trade-off and a short brief naming what stays constant. Ask which hesitation to address first. The owner supplies claim evidence; a research or campaign lead can gather audience response. A prototype leaves campaign effectiveness unresolved.
