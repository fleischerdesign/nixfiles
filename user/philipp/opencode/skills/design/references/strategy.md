# Strategic Design

## When to load & job

Load this for a decision brief about where to focus, what outcome to pursue, or what to stop doing. The artifact makes competing directions and their opportunity costs discussable before a sponsor commits scarce resources. Use business design when the open question is who pays or how an exchange could sustain the offer; use service design when the direction is set and the open question is how delivery works across roles and channels. This is a briefing and review framework, not a validated strategy or a mandate to redirect work.

## Inputs & concepts

Get the decision owner and horizon from the sponsor, current commitments and discretionary capacity from portfolio or delivery leads, and evidence about the intended outcome from research or operations. Ask which commitments have already been made externally and who can reopen them. When an objective is contested, set out whose outcome each frame serves; do not resolve the disagreement in the wording. When capacity is missing, name the competing use of the same people rather than inventing an available budget.

An **outcome** is a changed condition for a named group; a **lever** is something the organization can change to influence it. A **strategic choice** commits effort to one mechanism over another, with a corresponding exclusion. A **causal premise** explains why the lever might change the outcome here. **Opportunity cost** is the benefit or option forgone when an allocation is made, not just the money spent. A **binding constraint** removes an option from the owner's remit; a preference is a reason to favor it or not. The brief should say who has authority over either.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Outcome frame versus committed means.** Frame around the outcome when the sponsor can change the mechanism. If a contract fixes the means, isolate the part of the choice still open. Put a wider frame alongside the narrower one when it exposes an intervention the sponsor could take to another decision owner.
- **Reversible move versus commitment now.** A bounded allocation can answer a pivotal question while retaining other options. If a procurement or staffing window would close while the team gathers evidence, show what committing preserves and what it displaces.
- **Reach versus speed.** A reusable intervention may eventually serve more groups, while concentrating effort on one group may change its outcome sooner. Agree which outcome and time horizon matter before presenting favorites; show the conflict instead of averaging it away.

1. Write a decision sentence that names the owner, the outcome, the population, and the commitment the owner is being asked to make. Name any authority that sits elsewhere. Where objectives diverge, keep the alternative sentence visible.
2. For building structurally different alternatives against a meaningful current course, load [Option framing](knowledge/option-framing.md) (`references/knowledge/option-framing.md`). Carry that comparison into an **allocation-and-premise sheet**. For each direction, record the scarce role or budget it draws on, the work it would pause, and the causal premise linking the allocation to the outcome.
3. Bring the sheet to the relevant owners to annotate what is already known and what originates in the proposal. Translate a pivotal premise into a concrete evidence request: what cases to examine, who holds them, and which interpretation would change the allocation. Distinguish information that can arrive before commitment from information only a bounded commitment could produce.
4. Compose the brief around the choice, not the workshop history. If the evidence favors one direction under stated conditions, make the preference conditional and name what would change it. Put the choice to continue on the same page, including the commitments it preserves.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

In an illustrative onboarding brief, an executive asks for a learning portal. The wider outcome is that new hires can handle a routine case with less assistance. A portal and protected practice time draw on the same experienced staff: one for authoring, the other for coaching. The allocation sheet exposes the less obvious comparison: authoring postpones updates to the existing case guide; coaching temporarily takes senior staff out of case handling. If recent cases show that guidance exists but novices cannot apply it to ambiguous situations, protect practice time first. If necessary instructions are scattered and experienced staff also spend time locating them, consolidation may address the bottleneck. The training and operations leads can reconstruct recent cases; neither direction is established by the brief.

Treating the launch as the objective produces a choice between portal variants and hides both alternatives and displaced work. Retain the launch as a constraint only if its owner says it is fixed; even then, ask how much staff time is still open to allocation.

## Standards, limits & handover

Richard Rumelt, *Good Strategy/Bad Strategy* (2011), chapter 5, “The Kernel of Good Strategy,” supplies a method lens: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action. It is not a norm. Record contract terms or funding limits as project specifications only when their owners supply them. The sheet's columns are a working convention; the onboarding allocations are example choices, not benchmarks. A shared document with an adjacent comparison sheet is sufficient; keep evidence references attached to the premise they inform.

Show the sponsor the decision brief, the allocation sheet, and the evidence requests. Research can investigate the outcome mechanism; operational owners can establish availability and displaced work; the sponsor chooses whether to commit. Ask which commitment can move, who owns the constraint, and what evidence would change the choice.
