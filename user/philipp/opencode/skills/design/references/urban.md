# Urban

## When to load & job

Use this for a street, square, district, or public-space brief whose effects reach beyond the site boundary. Compare scenarios across scales and name who uses, owns, and maintains the space. Architecture leads when the primary question is the program of a single building.

## Inputs

Gather current network and parcel maps, land ownership, transit and loading patterns, maintenance responsibilities, and documented observations at different times. Request current data from the relevant authorities and local accounts from people who use the place. If evidence is missing, label routes and activity patterns as hypotheses rather than drawing them as established demand.

## Decisions & process

- Start at the district scale when the brief promises a new connection. A direct crossing can reconnect destinations on opposite sides of a corridor, but it changes how that corridor operates. Improving an existing crossing concentrates work at a known point but may preserve a long detour. Map the whole walk from origin to destination before drawing the curb.
- Decide which uses need the same frontage at different times. Dedicated seating gives a square a dependable place to linger; a clear edge can accommodate market servicing but offers less everyday comfort. Draw an ordinary morning, a market setup, and the period after pack-down.
- If responsibility is split between agencies or owners, compare improvements within one party's control with a more continuous scheme that depends on several parties. The smaller option may be easier to maintain but stop where the experience matters most. Overlay responsibility boundaries on the proposed routes.

## Typical mistakes & examples

A plaza concept adds seating where a midday site visit found spare space. That same edge is used for early deliveries, so the concept displaces loading onto a neighboring block. Plot the delivery round and the public-space activity by time before deciding whether to separate them or share the edge.

## Deliverables & questions for the human

Show a network map, street-scale scenarios, and a map of affected actors and responsibilities. Ask which trip or use the project should improve and who would maintain the space. Local users contribute lived evidence; planners and transport specialists bring field observations and assess network effects. Planning approval, street changes, and implementation commitments do not follow from the study.
