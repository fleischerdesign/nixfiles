# Urban

## When to load & job

Use this for a street, square, district, or public-space brief whose effects reach beyond the site boundary. Compare scenarios at network, block, and street scale; show who uses, controls, and maintains the affected space. Architecture leads when the main question is the program of a single building. This is a briefing and review artifact for local users, planners, transport specialists, and operators, not a street design for implementation.

## Inputs & concepts

Get current network and parcel maps, ownership and public access information, transit and loading patterns, and maintenance responsibilities from the relevant authorities and operators. Ask local users about ordinary and exceptional trips. Collect observations with times and dates, including when the space sets up, changes hands, or empties. Notice gaps: the lack of people crossing at a location may reflect a barrier rather than lack of demand. Label inferred links and demand as hypotheses rather than upgrading them to observations.

An **origin–destination pair** describes a whole trip, not just movement along the block in view. A **desire line** is an observed or inferred direct connection; it is not a recommendation for a particular crossing. A **frontage** is an active edge with doors, servicing, waiting, or occupation, not merely the face of a parcel. The **right-of-way**, ownership, operating control, and routine maintenance may have different boundaries. Draw them separately when the distinction determines who must participate.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Direct connection versus concentrated change.** A new crossing could reconnect destinations across a corridor, but it changes how that corridor operates. Upgrading an existing crossing concentrates work at a known point while retaining some detours.
- **Dedicated occupation versus time-sharing.** Seating gives a square an everyday place to linger; a clear frontage can support market servicing at the cost of everyday comfort. Time-sharing depends on someone setting up, clearing, and managing the edge.
- **One-owner scope versus continuity.** A scheme within one owner's control may be easier to maintain. A continuous route can improve the whole trip but relies on commitments on both sides of a boundary.

1. Draw the existing trips at network scale before locating a curb or piece of furniture. Separate observed routes from inferred ones and include alternatives beyond the intervention block. Choose the same origin and destination for the baseline and each scenario.
2. Make a **frontage-by-time ledger** for the edges where uses meet. Record the door or location, actor, use, time window, neighboring activity, and who controls the space. Use [Spatial programming](knowledge/spatial-programming.md) (`references/knowledge/spatial-programming.md`) where operator activities need translating into public-space zones and relationships; use the ledger to keep off-site dependencies and changes over the day visible.
3. Translate each scenario into a block plan and a street section at the difficult edge. Trace which movement shifts next door or moves to another time. Overlay maintenance and control boundaries; annotate each change with the party whose decision it depends on.
4. If the proposed trip needs guidance, use [Wayfinding](knowledge/wayfinding.md) (`references/knowledge/wayfinding.md`) to locate decisions and recovery on the actual approaches. A cue can explain an available link; it cannot make an unfunded or unavailable connection usable. Show the trip from where people start, not just from inside the new square.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A square looks underused at noon, so a sketch puts fixed seating along the shop edge. Early observations show that vans stop there before the shops open. The decisive ledger entry is not simply “loading, morning”: a driver unloads at the service door, and the handler takes goods from vehicle to door on that side of the block. Moving the stop around the corner puts the trolley on a pedestrian approach and moves the vehicle activity to a neighboring frontage. One option puts permanent seating away from the working edge. Another retains shared frontage but marks who moves the seating after deliveries and where it is kept. Show both the morning handover and the noon use so local operators can say whether the assumed sharing is realistic.

A midday photograph treated as the whole operating brief can make a design export conflict beyond the view. Follow the activity to its next location and represent both the displaced trip and the new use.

## Standards, limits & handover

In the U.S., the FHWA *Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices*, 11th edition (2023), Part 3 (markings) and Part 4 (highway traffic signals), is a primary source for traffic-control devices, not a warrant for the urban concept itself. `Verify` the applicable revision, local adoption, and provisions with the responsible authority. A frontage booking window is a project operating specification, ledger colors are conventions, and the square scenario is illustrative. A GIS or layered map can display jurisdiction and time overlays without treating mapped parcel lines as a survey.

Show the network map, street scenarios, and affected actors and responsibilities. Ask which trip or use matters most and who would maintain the space. Local users contribute lived evidence; planners and transport specialists bring field observations and assess network effects. No planning approval, street change, or implementation commitment follows from the study.
