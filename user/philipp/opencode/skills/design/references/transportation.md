# Transportation

## When to load & job

Use this for a vehicle or mobility concept whose layout must serve an entire trip: approaching, boarding, riding or driving, loading, and servicing. Produce a comparative package study and an operating storyboard so the vehicle team can see which tasks the concept prioritizes. Let an interaction module lead if the sole question is the behavior of an already located interface. Here, interface questions matter because posture, sightlines, and movement depend on the package. This is a concept brief, not vehicle engineering.

## Inputs & concepts

Ask the operator for the **duty cycle**: not just trip length but the sequence and frequency of stops, boarding and loading episodes, changeovers, and service. Collect the expected users, aids, equipment and loads, curb or platform conditions, and who does each task. Ask the vehicle team for coordinate datums, candidate platform drawings, package envelopes, and permitted ranges of adjustment or motion. When these are unavailable, draw relative zones and symbolic edges instead of assigning clearances.

A **hard point** is a location treated as fixed for the variant, such as a chassis-provided mounting point; distinguish it from convenient geometry that is still negotiable. Static space for a bag is different from the space required to turn with it, and both differ from the path needed to remove a service part. In section, ground, curb, doorway and vehicle floor must be shown together: a clear aisle says nothing about getting into it. Treat a passenger count as a project input, not a proxy for all simultaneous bodies, loads and motions.

## Decisions & procedure

- Frequent curbside stops favor door-side space for circulation and stowage. A longer ride may favor more seats there, giving up room for people and gear to move together.
- A shared bay can absorb changing passenger and cargo mixes but adds a changeover and someone must do it. Dedicated zones simplify each stop but consume volume even when empty.
- Separate driving actions from parked setup. A distinct control may reduce menu navigation, but it occupies space in an already crowded view or reach zone. Leave assessment of attention, reach, and feedback to human factors.

1. Make a **trip-state package sheet**. Give each row a trip state, location, concurrent users and loads, next movement, operator role, and unresolved geometry. Include the busy stop, settled ride and service reset. Distinguish simultaneous activities from activities that could be sequenced only if someone controls the order.
2. Use [Spatial programming](knowledge/spatial-programming.md) (`references/knowledge/spatial-programming.md`) to turn activities into zone relationships and separate flows. Translate the consequential relationships into two package options anchored to the same platform datums. Mark fixed hardware, adjustable zones and unknowns differently.
3. Select the states that drive each option. Use [Fit envelopes](knowledge/fit-envelopes.md) (`references/knowledge/fit-envelopes.md`) to compare people, loads and moving parts in coordinated plan and section. Carry the conflicts into a storyboard, including whether the driver is moving or parked when a proposed action occurs. Annotate each conflict with who can supply the missing geometry or observation.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

An illustrative airport shuttle picks up passengers with wheeled luggage. Both candidate layouts have the same door and a clear aisle in the settled ride. In the busy-stop row, an arriving passenger turns a bag into the rack while a second approaches the step. The bag and its handler occupy the landing at the same time. Rotating the rack to open toward the cabin could keep the landing clear of the loading action but brings that action into the seated area; a managed one-at-a-time approach keeps the original rack but transfers work to dispatch or the driver. Draw both in the stop frame. The typical mistake is treating the empty aisle outline as the boarding path: the outline excludes the very task that consumes it.

## Standards, limits & handover

ISO 7250-1:2017 defines body measurements and landmarks, not suitable reach or vehicle clearances; `verify` applicable edition and section. For a U.S. transportation project, 49 CFR Part 38, Subpart B addresses buses, vans and systems; `verify` applicability and provisions with the vehicle team before treating any requirement as relevant. The platform drawing and operator duty cycle are project specifications; state labels are a study convention and the shuttle is illustrative.

Show scenario boards, paired package sketches and the unresolved safety and interface questions. Vehicle engineers supply platform and packaging evidence; human-factors specialists observe representative tasks in a mockup. Dimensions, safety conclusions and engineering decisions stay outside the study. Ask which loads and passengers must coexist, and at what boarding location.
