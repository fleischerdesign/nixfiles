# Information Architecture

## When to load & job

Use this to organize content and destinations so people can predict where to look. Work on categories, relationships, navigation, and labels before drawing visual treatments. UX takes over once the main question is how someone completes a task at a destination.

## Inputs

Start with a content inventory, current navigation and URLs, known destinations, and the words people use in search or support. Use actual records or documentation to establish what belongs together. If there is no evidence about mental models, mark proposed groupings and labels as hypotheses and show where existing items would land.

## Decisions & process

Choose an organizing principle for sibling categories. Task labels such as “Replace a card” give people with a known intent a direct entry; object labels such as “Cards” gather related options for people who want to understand what is available. Compare two text-only trees with the same destinations: the choice changes what somebody has to know at the first fork.

Choose a canonical home separately from alternate entrances. If a destination has one clear owner but is sought in several places, link to the same page from those places. This keeps updates in one place but can make the visitor's route differ from the breadcrumb; use the breadcrumb to show the destination's home. When attributes overlap across many items, consider filters instead of adding near-duplicate branches.

Use the narrowest label people can predict. A precise domain term helps specialists distinguish neighboring categories; a plain-language label may be easier to recognize but less exact. Keep synonyms in search or alternate links rather than offering two indistinguishable entries.

## Typical mistakes & examples

Putting “Change my address” under “Operations” mirrors ownership but makes people guess who runs the service. Give it a task entry under “Personal details” and keep ownership in the content record.

## Deliverables & questions for the human

Show the proposed tree, representative content mapped to destinations, and routes from search or cross-links. Ask which distinctions people already make in their own words and which existing links must continue to resolve.
