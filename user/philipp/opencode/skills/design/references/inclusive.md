# Inclusive Design

## When to load & job

Use this alongside the lead deliverable when a design assumes people can perceive, understand, act, or recover in one particular way. Translate exclusion into requirements across the experience, not a label attached to the finished artifact.

## Inputs

Bring the task, use contexts, existing accounts of barriers, and the project's stated standards. Map permanent, temporary, and situational constraints to specific interactions: someone with one hand, an injured wrist, or a bag in hand may all struggle with a two-handed action for different reasons. Without firsthand evidence, mark these as scenarios to explore, not accounts of what people experience.

## Decisions & process

- Prefer one flexible path when the same adaptation can serve the task without extra setup. Provide a parallel route when the main medium imposes a barrier you can't remove. That route adds maintenance and can become second-class; show whether it preserves privacy, timing, and the ability to finish independently.
- Carry the requirement through the whole task. If someone can submit a form without a pointer but can't correct an error the same way, focus on recovery rather than adding another way to start. Show the handoff between interface, messages, and support, and name who owns each part.
- Choose participation formats around the people the current design leaves out. An asynchronous walkthrough gives people control over pacing but makes follow-up slower; a supported live session lets the researcher observe adaptations but requires scheduling and can change how people act. Budget for the accommodations participants request.

## Typical mistakes & examples

Putting time-sensitive instructions only in a station announcement makes them unavailable to some deaf travelers and easy to miss in a noisy concourse. Carry the same instruction into the departure display at the point where travelers choose a platform, not just into a transcript elsewhere.

## Deliverables & questions for the human

Show annotated task variants and requirements for each deliverable, with unresolved barriers named. Participants need to try the real task in context, and a specialist can interpret applicable standards for the implemented work. Where must people be able to finish without asking for help? Which teams can keep alternate routes current?
