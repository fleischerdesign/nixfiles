# Film & Video

## When to load & job

Use this for a storyboard, animatic, or edit direction. Your material is the sequence: what viewers learn from one shot, what they infer from the next, and how long the idea has to land. This is previsualization, not a shoot or finished film. Let title design lead when the words on screen are the event, or VFX lead when the unresolved decision is how to build a particular shot.

## Inputs & concepts

Bring the script or interview selects, transcripts linked to source clips, available footage, intended audience, and producer or distributor brief. Get runtime, frame rate, aspect ratios, version requirements, and any locked picture or lines from editorial and production. Without footage, use labeled boards with the missing coverage named. Without delivery specs, state the working runtime and frame shape; exact shot selection and delivery versions remain open.

A **beat** is a change in the viewer's understanding or expectation, not necessarily a shot change. **Coverage** is material the edit can cut among: an establishing view, action, reverse angle, reaction, or detail. A **cutaway** shows material outside the current framing; it can add information but can also suggest an unintended causal relationship. Source positions locate footage; sequence positions locate its use in the cut. A **split edit** gives picture and sound separate boundaries. When the outgoing voice carries over incoming picture, the new image can reframe what the voice means, even though the wording stays intact.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Orient or withhold.** Open wide when geography explains the action. Enter on a detail when curiosity matters more, then supply a locating shot. Compare both openings through the same reveal, not as isolated first frames.
- **Stay with the action or cut to its consequence.** Hold a gesture when how it is done tells the story. Cut to the recipient when their reaction changes its meaning; the audience may then miss how the gesture ends.
- **Pace by thought or by impact.** Let a voice finish over the next image when that image helps the audience complete the thought. Cut picture and sound together for rupture; used throughout, this gives every beat the same emphasis.

1. Mark the point in the script or selects where each audience inference becomes possible. Keep qualifications attached to their claims. List the shot or audible evidence that would actually support each inference, and call out evidence the crew still needs to supply.
2. Build a **paper edit** with beat, shot ID or board ID, source excerpt, sequence placement, dialogue, and intended inference. Give picture and sound their own rows so you can move a cut without moving a line. For placing arrivals and holds around the important beats, use [Motion timing](knowledge/motion-timing.md) (`references/knowledge/motion-timing.md`). For spotting sound against those edits, use [Sound cues](knowledge/sound-cues.md) (`references/knowledge/sound-cues.md`).
3. Turn the paper edit into a timed animatic: place the real dialogue or a labeled temp read, trim boards or footage to show the intended durations, and retain shot IDs for discussion. Use alternate frames for aspect changes that alter geography or conceal a reveal, not just a center crop. For interpreting mixed camera or render sources in the review cut, use [Color management](knowledge/color-management.md) (`references/knowledge/color-management.md`).

## Worked example & typical mistakes

In an interview about a pump, the speaker says, “We thought the motor had failed … but the intake was blocked.” The paper edit keeps the interview image through the pause and “but,” then brings in the intake image while the sentence finishes. Showing the motor on the pause would temporarily give the false diagnosis visual authority; showing the intake before “but” gives away the correction. If the only intake shot is illustrative stock, mark it as such rather than letting the sequence imply it depicts the actual incident. Cutting on every pause turns unfinished thoughts into apparent conclusions; move cuts to changes of meaning instead.

## Standards, limits & handover

[SMPTE ST 12-1:2014](https://doi.org/10.5594/SMPTE.ST12-1.2014), *Time and Control Code*, defines timecode representations; `verify` the applicable revision and frame-counting mode with editorial before exchanging frame addresses. Timecode does not prescribe editorial pacing. If the distributor specifies HDTV parameters, [ITU-R BT.709-6](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BT.709/en) describes that system; the delivery owner specifies the actual output settings, not the animatic author.

Show the sequence in context, not just the boards, with shot IDs, dialogue and temp sound identified. Ask what viewers should know before the reveal and which moment deserves the longest hold. Editorial's next artifact is a rough cut from actual footage; production resolves missing coverage and delivery variants.
