# Editorial

## When to load & job

Use this for books, journals, reports, and long-form screen publications. Shape the reading rhythm across a sequence: where readers settle in, where they pause, and how they find their place again. Typography handles the setting of individual text roles; Print handles the printer’s artwork requirements.

## Inputs

Use the manuscript, section hierarchy, representative images and captions, notes, and the publication’s house style. Get the trim and binding from production for print; get the intended screen formats and reflow requirements from the publishing brief. If copy or formats are provisional, lay out one representative section and say which pagination or reflow decisions remain open.

## Decisions & process

- **Grid for the dominant reading mode.** Give continuous prose one main measure; use a modular grid when readers move repeatedly among text, tables, and images. Independent modules make references easier to find but can interrupt an argument. Compare a prose-heavy spread with a figure-heavy one; let modules stack in reading order on narrow screens.
- **Rhythm through a section.** Make chapter openings a deliberate change of pace, then return to a repeatable body layout. A full-bleed opener buys a stronger pause but takes space from the text and can push a key image away from its reference. Lay out the opener, a middle spread, and the transition to the next section.
- **Orientation without clutter.** Use running heads and folios where readers need to flip back or cite a page. An opener can omit the running head to give the title room. In reflowing editions, use section anchors and a linked contents list rather than promising stable screen page numbers.

## Typical mistakes & examples

Building the grid around the cover story’s short captions makes a later two-paragraph caption collide with the next column. Give captions a repeatable area that can grow, even if it means reducing the image on the busiest spread.

## Deliverables & questions for the human

Show consecutive pages and the equivalent screen sequence with real headings, captions, and notes. Ask whether readers mainly read straight through or return to cite sections, and whether print and screen must share a reference scheme.
