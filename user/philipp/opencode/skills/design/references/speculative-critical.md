# Speculative & Critical Design

## When to load & job

Use this to brief a fictional scenario and a situated artifact that invite people to question a present assumption. A concept prototype makes a proposed product discussable; this study makes a rule or value discussable, even if no one should build the object it depicts. Deliver a scenario card, an encounter prototype, and a facilitation brief, not a roadmap, prediction, or claim about how audiences will respond. State what the work is arguing before choosing its form.

## Inputs & concepts

Collect the practice or belief under question, a documented rule or example that grounds it, the intended audience, and the venue’s constraints. Ask the curator or host whether visitors encounter it alone, can share images outside the venue, or have time for conversation. Ask a domain contact which institutions and procedures must remain recognizable. If the venue is undecided, script one assumed encounter and say what would change in another setting.

The **point of departure** is the rule that changes; the **scenario** describes the world in which that rule operates. An **artifact from that world** is what someone would actually receive or use there, such as a receipt or rejected appeal. Let its fields, defaults, or omissions reveal who has standing and who does not. **Framing** is how a real audience learns what is fictional; distinguish it from how a fictional recipient is instructed to act. A plausible-looking artifact can make a premise legible without making it likely.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Near or distant departure.** Changing one rule in a familiar setting helps an audience trace consequences. A more distant world can unsettle a deeper assumption, but every unfamiliar institution competes for the reader’s attention.
- **Familiar form or overt fiction.** A recognizable notice lets people read the details as consequential, but can be mistaken for a real service. An overt frame reduces confusion, at some cost to surprise. Do not depend on a wall label if the object is likely to circulate alone.
- **Explicit contradiction or unresolved tension.** A short encounter may need the contradiction in the artifact itself. A facilitated group can work with ambiguity if a prompt helps people name what they disagree about without prescribing the conclusion.

1. Write the present assumption and the question that challenges it. Use [Option framing](knowledge/option-framing.md) (`references/knowledge/option-framing.md`) if you need to expose the baseline assumption and compare scenario premises. Use the contrast to clarify the provocation, not to recommend a scenario.
2. Draft a **scenario card** with documented anchors, the invented rule, what stays ordinary, the institution administering the rule, and a concrete consequence for someone affected. Name what remains unexplained instead of adding a history of the entire world.
3. Choose the encounter where the consequence becomes unavoidable. Annotate the object with what each detail reveals: who sent it, what choice it offers, what action it refuses, and what happens after the recipient responds. Mock up enough of the document or interface to make that sequence readable.
4. Script the real encounter from first contact to discussion. Decide where the fiction is disclosed in the venue and on the object. Give the facilitator prompts about whose choices are visible, who can contest the rule, and where an equivalent assumption operates today.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

Imagine a city that sells quiet street hours. The argument is about who controls public space, not whether the booking software is convincing. On the scenario card, keep the ordinary municipal complaint process but invent a rule that only a permit holder can contest the scheduling of a quiet window. Pair a purchase receipt with a denial to a night-shift worker. On the denial, show the reason for refusal and who is entitled to request a review. That line, not a more elaborate city logo, expresses the change in standing. Give both documents a visible fiction label; ask the audience how its reading changes between receipt and refusal.

A glossy interactive demo alone can read as a pitch and move discussion to pricing. Shift the encounter from checkout to the affected person’s attempt to object, where the proposed rule’s distribution of authority becomes visible.

## Standards, limits & handover

Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby, *Speculative Everything: Design, Fiction, and Social Dreaming* (MIT Press, 2013), chs. 1–2, introduces the speculative stance and the relation between design and fiction. It is a methodological source, not a standard for credibility or audience response. The scenario card is a working convention; the venue supplies disclosure, display, participation, and distribution constraints. A labeled mock document or storyboard is enough to stage the encounter. Do not use real forms or live contact routes in a way that invites mistaken action; `verify` any venue or rights restrictions with the host.

Show the human the card, object in context, argument, and prompts. A domain specialist can challenge the grounding; a facilitator can hear how actual audiences interpret the encounter. Neither makes it a prediction. What assumption must be hardest to ignore, and how much ambiguity can the venue support?
