# Exhibition

## When to load & job

Use this for an exhibition narrative and layout: what visitors encounter first, what draws them closer, and what they carry into the next room. Lead with interior if the question is how the venue works between shows rather than how this story unfolds. Deliver a visitor storyboard, an annotated layout, and viewing and media studies for the exhibition team, not casework, mount, or rigging documents.

## Inputs & concepts

Get the curatorial premise, actual object list and interpretive material, lender and conservation conditions, measured venue plan, expected visiting patterns, and media operating assumptions. Ask the registrar and conservator what may be displayed, for how long, and under what conditions. Ask venue staff where groups pause, what can be bypassed, and how rooms are turned over. If the venue or loan is unsettled, label proxy objects and openings and leave object placement and case geometry open.

A **narrative beat** is a change in what the visitor understands, not merely a room title. A **dwell point** is where a person occupies space while reading, watching, or listening; the group attending it can become a moving obstruction. A **sightline** joins a particular approach or viewing position to a particular target; a clean view from the center of the room says little about the first reveal. **Interpretive hierarchy** distinguishes the premise needed for a short visit from detail available to a lingering visitor. Graphics, objects, and media can take different roles within that hierarchy.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Sequence versus independent clusters.** Use ordered encounters when an argument depends on what came before. Give clusters an opening idea of their own when visitors arrive midway or skip rooms, accepting less control over the reveal.
- **Prominent dwell versus a clear through-route.** A film or detailed object on the main axis is immediately noticed; a side pocket allows others to continue but needs a reason to turn toward it.
- **Timed media versus self-paced interpretation.** Projection can show a mechanism in motion but can gather an audience at a particular moment and affect neighboring displays through sound or light. Stills can explain phases at the visitor's pace while giving up continuous motion.

1. Make an **encounter strip** with one row per beat: intended takeaway, object or medium, what a visitor already knows, where they are likely to stop, and what still works if they skip the row. Trace a full and a short visit; put any irreplaceable premise on a route both can encounter.
2. Set the strip against the venue. Use [Spatial programming](knowledge/spatial-programming.md) (`references/knowledge/spatial-programming.md`) to relate display, dwell, through-movement, and staff operations. Translate narrative dependency into placement only where it changes what visitors need to reach or see next.
3. Use [Wayfinding](knowledge/wayfinding.md) (`references/knowledge/wayfinding.md`) where shortcuts and room choices need cues, not as a substitute for a coherent sequence. Mark the bypass for optional media and show where it rejoins the story.
4. Use [Scene studies](knowledge/scene-studies.md) (`references/knowledge/scene-studies.md`) for viewpoints where case, crowd, and graphics may mask one another. Compare the layouts from the same arrival and viewing positions. Attach media and fabrication questions to the beat that depends on them rather than treating the images as display specifications.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A rare mechanical object anchors the first room, and a film explains what moves inside it. The encounter strip shows that “see the object” is indispensable but “follow the motion continuously” is optional. In the first sketch a knot of standing viewers at the film masks the case for new arrivals. In the alternative the case is the first target and the film occupies a side pocket with a route that rejoins the story beyond the case. Use the same approach views with proxy viewers in both versions; include the position of someone who cannot see over a standing group. A still sequence beside the object offers a second treatment if the pocket competes with the next exhibit. The curator can judge what the short visit communicates without having to watch the film.

Making a timed introduction unavoidable makes someone else's dwell time determine access to the object. Separate the story dependency from the media choice before fixing the placement.

## Standards, limits & handover

[CIE 157:2004](https://cie.co.at/publications/control-damage-museum-objects-optical-radiation), *Control of Damage to Museum Objects by Optical Radiation*, is technical guidance for optical radiation and objects, not an object-specific exposure allowance; `verify` applicable edition and section with the conservator. For U.S. projects, the [2010 ADA Standards](https://www.ada.gov/law-and-regs/design-standards/2010-stds/) §§216 and 703 concern sign scoping and technical provisions; `verify` applicability rather than treating every label as a scoped sign. Lender conditions are project specifications. Proxy figures and encounter-strip labels are conventions; the mechanical object is illustrative.

Show the storyboard, short route, sightline views, and open media and fabrication decisions. Ask what visitors must encounter even if they skip part of the show. The curator and conservator supply object requirements; the exhibition designer and fabricator provide full-scale viewing and display mock-ups. Final case, mount, and rigging details and fabrication remain outside the study.
