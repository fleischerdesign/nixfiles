# Print

## When to load & job

Lead with Print for a poster, postcard, folded leaflet, or other artwork whose design depends on the finished sheet. Show what readers see at the finished edge, in the hand, and through any sequence of folds. Deliver editable artwork and the agreed exchange file when the production specification exists; otherwise deliver a dimensioned study and a brief for the printer. Use Editorial when the main problem is the rhythm or navigation of a publication, not how the artwork gets onto the sheet.

## Inputs & concepts

Bring final or labeled provisional copy, original images, brand assets, and a description of how the piece is displayed, opened or held. Get finished and flat dimensions, sidedness, folds, stock, coating, ink plan and file requirements from the printer. Ask for any finishing diagram that identifies fold direction and panel widths; do not assume that a panel tucked inside a roll fold is as wide as the others.

**Trim** is the intended finished edge; **bleed** is expendable artwork outside that edge; **safe area** is an inset for material that must survive ordinary variation. A crease can need its own clearance even well inside the trim. Distinguish a page, a reader spread and an imposed sheet: a pair of pages designed together does not necessarily travel together in the printer's file. Treat an assumed format as a study, not as authority for finishing or color.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Bleed or border.** A full-bleed image makes the surface feel continuous but sacrifices some control over the exact crop. A border preserves the image frame; a narrow border makes shifts in the cut more apparent. Compare both at finished size.
- **Panel or panorama.** Give a headline or contact block one face if it must read with the piece closed. A continuous background can connect the opened spread, at the cost of a partial image in the closed view.
- **Atmosphere or shadow separation.** If detail matters on the specified stock, compare a locally opened shadow treatment with the denser original. The lighter treatment may make the subject easier to distinguish but change the mood.

1. Draw an **annotated flat and handling sequence**. Label side, reading top, cut, fold and visible face in each state; assign each copy block to the first state in which it needs to be read. Fold a marked paper dummy before committing the relationship between the front and reverse.
2. Place the artwork against that map. Position faces and captions at finished size first, then solve the edge with a crop or extension. Carry only visual elements that can lose information over a crease. Build the exchange geometry and export settings using [Print PDF](knowledge/print-pdf.md) (`references/knowledge/print-pdf.md`), so dimensions, marks and pages versus spreads come from the printer rather than the layout preset.
3. Identify the stock-sensitive decisions and translate them into a proof request. Use [Color management](knowledge/color-management.md) (`references/knowledge/color-management.md`) to assign conversion responsibility and describe the printing condition. Keep the source edit separate from the destination treatment so the same image can be prepared for another stock later.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A museum leaflet opens from a portrait cover to a wide landscape. On the dummy, the ticket information only appears after the second fold opens, although it needs to be read at the counter. Move admission and opening hours onto the first revealed panel and keep the full address on the back. On the annotated flat, mark the sequence as closed, first opening, and fully open. Crop the landscape so a stretch of water crosses the crease rather than the boat that anchors the image. The closed object now works as a quick reference; the fully opened view still has a continuous horizon.

A dark background stopped at trim can leave a white sliver when the cut shifts out. Extend the background alone into the supplied bleed; enlarging the entire composition may put the newly moved ticket text too close to the cut.

## Standards, limits & handover

[ISO 15930-7:2010, edition 2](https://www.iso.org/standard/55843.html) specifies PDF/X-4 and PDF/X-4p exchange; `verify` the relevant clause and requested variant. It does not supply the job's bleed, fold allowance or production tolerances. For InDesign use Adobe's [Adobe PDF export options](https://helpx.adobe.com/indesign/desktop/save-export-and-publish/save-and-export/adobe-pdf-export-options.html), “Marks and Bleeds” and “Output”; `verify` the installed version. A full-bleed layout is a design convention, not an exchange requirement.

Show the artwork at trim size, the annotated flat and the handling sequence. Ask what must remain visible when closed. In the proof request, name the stock and the image areas or solids worth comparing. The printer supplies the physical proof; the human and printer decide whether to proceed.
