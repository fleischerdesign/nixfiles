# Physical Product (Industrial)

## When to load & job

Use this for the brief behind a handheld tool, appliance, or other physical product. Work out what the object has to do in someone's hands and which constraints should shape it. Packaging leads when the question is how the product is presented for sale, not how it works.

## Inputs

Ask for the task sequence, users and use environment, mating parts, cleaning and service routines, and intended production volumes. Take interface dimensions from the owner's drawings or measured samples; ask the manufacturing partner for process and material limits. With neither, use relative geometry in an annotated study and leave fit and material selection open.

## Decisions & process

- Start with the hardest hand movement. If a refill must be changed one-handed, show where the object is braced and where the other hand would otherwise go. If compact storage matters more, a smaller body may be worth a two-handed change. Sketch the whole gesture, not just the grip.
- Put the enclosure seam where it makes sense for the product's life. A removable housing can make wear parts easier to reach, at the cost of exposed seams and more places for dirt to collect. A less interrupted exterior can make wipe-down easier but complicate service. Compare the cleaning path with the service sequence.
- Treat material and process as paired questions. For uncertain demand, ask about lower-tooling routes that may cost more per unit. For stable repeat volumes, ask what geometry a tooling investment would commit the team to. Put those dependencies beside each concept.

## Typical mistakes & examples

A dispenser is drawn with its refill door on the rear because the front looks cleaner. Installed against a wall, it has to be moved every time it is refilled. Draw the refill motion in the installed position and compare side access with front access.

## Deliverables & questions for the human

Show an annotated use sequence, two concept sketches, and open material and process questions. Ask who changes the refill and what may move during service. An industrial designer provides a handling mock-up with representative users; the manufacturing partner provides process evidence and samples. Final dimensions, material specification, and fabrication remain outside this handoff.
