# Interior

## When to load & job

Use this for a room or fit-out brief: what happens where, how people move, and how light, sound, and finishes support those activities. Show the room when its uses overlap, not as an empty composition. Bring in architecture if the decision changes the building envelope or relationships between floors. This is a layout and finish-intent study for the project team, not a partition or services design.

## Inputs & concepts

Ask for a measured plan and sections, dated photos at useful times of day, fixed service points, movable equipment, and operator accounts of arrivals, work, cleaning, and reset. Request the location and swing of openings and the position of existing obstructions, not just the room perimeter. Ask staff when a queue forms, how a trolley moves, and which conversations need separation. With photographs but no survey, use a relationship diagram and mark openings and service points as provisional.

A **zone** groups compatible activities rather than assigning a style to a patch of floor. A **circulation band** is space used for travel in a defined operating state; it is not the negative space left around furniture symbols. A seat in use, open drawer, or queue can change that state. **Finish transitions** belong at changes in exposure or upkeep: the dry edge on a photo may not be where wet footwear or trolley traffic actually stops. Daylight is directional and time-dependent; show the seated view and screen orientation before equating more window area with better task light.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Direct spine versus edge route.** A central aisle makes frequent destinations legible but may divide work or conversation. An edge route can protect an activity while lengthening trips or making the destination less visible.
- **Window-side work versus shared daylight.** Assign the view to desks when it supports long occupation and glare can be addressed; give it to shared seating when that distributes the benefit or avoids putting screens in conflicting orientations. The latter puts more reliance on electric light at desks.
- **Continuous finish versus targeted transition.** Continuity can make a compact room feel coherent and simplify some cleaning routines. A targeted change can address wet, noisy, or heavily rolled-over areas, but its edge introduces a detailing and maintenance decision.

1. Define two operating snapshots with staff, such as a normal arrival and arrivals during a handover. Use [Spatial programming](knowledge/spatial-programming.md) (`references/knowledge/spatial-programming.md`) to turn operating episodes into zones and relationships.
2. Build an **occupied-room overlay** for each snapshot: put furniture in its actual operating position and trace visitor, staff, and cleaning movement separately. Mark where a conflict persists and where it belongs only to a short overlap. Take a section through the part that drives the layout, such as arrival, waiting, and counter.
3. Draw two zone layouts against the same fixed shell and services. Keep the same snapshots so a route is not made to look better by drawing fewer people. Add seated views toward the window or counter and annotate finish intent with the activity or exposure it serves.
4. Identify the moves that require specialist evidence: a changed partition may affect building services; a different ceiling treatment may change the acoustic question; a surface change may need the operator's cleaning method and the supplier's detailing. Keep those dependencies attached to the corresponding option.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

In a clinic waiting room, arrivals stack up when reception is dealing with an extended enquiry. Instead of drawing a generic queue along the counter, put its head where the conversation actually happens and draw the next visitor's intended waiting position. The route from the door now intersects the queue just as somebody draws a chair out into it. In one option, an inset seating bay removes the drawn-out chair from that intersection; in another, shifting the point where the queue starts leaves the seating edge in place but changes what newcomers see first. Compare the two from the entry and show how each affects the distance between the enquiry and the next person waiting. The queue length shown is a scenario, not a capacity specification.

An empty plan makes the residual strip look like circulation; the operator then encounters blocked movement at peak use. Carry the same occupied snapshot into both drawings instead of editing people out of the comparison.

## Standards, limits & handover

For a project subject to the U.S. DOJ [2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design](https://www.ada.gov/law-and-regs/design-standards/2010-stds/), §§206 and 402 address scoping and technical provisions for accessible routes; `verify` applicability and relevant provisions with the project specialist. These are normative where applicable, not universal aisle dimensions. Finish and cleaning requirements come from the project and supplier. The overlay's line styles are conventions and can be drawn on separate CAD or PDF layers over the survey base.

Show the zone program, occupied routes, and light and finish intentions in context. Ask which movements may cross and when glare or noise is most disruptive. The interior designer provides the survey basis and in-room mock-ups; lighting, acoustic, and services specialists address their respective implications. Finish, partition, and lighting specifications and construction decisions remain outside this study.
