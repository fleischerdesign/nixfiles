# Interior

## When to load & job

Use this for a room or fit-out brief: what happens where, how people move, and how light and finishes support those activities. If the question changes the building envelope or the relationship between floors, bring in architecture rather than treating that change as a furniture move.

## Inputs

Ask for a measured plan, photos at relevant times of day, fixed services, occupancy patterns, and cleaning routines. Get existing conditions from the survey team and operational constraints from the people who run the space. With photos but no survey, make a diagram of relationships rather than a dimensioned layout; mark openings and services as unconfirmed.

## Decisions & process

- Give frequent trips the direct route. A central aisle makes destinations obvious, but it can cut through the activities you are trying to support. A circulation edge protects work or conversation at the cost of a longer trip. Overlay visitor and staff paths during the busiest use you know about.
- Spend daylight where people benefit from it for the longest periods. Window-side desks can give workers a view but may put glare on screens; placing shared seating there spreads the benefit while leaving desks more dependent on electric light. Show seated viewpoints at the times that matter.
- Locate finish transitions by use, not just by color palette. A surface chosen for frequent cleaning at an entrance may be noisy in a consultation area. Draw the route between them and ask where wet shoes, rolling equipment, and quiet conversation actually overlap.

## Typical mistakes & examples

An empty waiting-room plan makes the route to reception look clear. Once someone pulls out a chair and a queue forms, arriving visitors squeeze through seated people. Overlay occupied furniture and a queue before comparing an inset waiting area with one along the route.

## Deliverables & questions for the human

Show a program by zone, circulation overlays, and light and finish intentions in context. Ask which movements may cross and when glare or noise is most disruptive. The interior designer brings the site survey and in-room layout and finish mock-ups; lighting and services specialists assess their implications. Partition, lighting, and finish specifications and any construction decisions remain outside the study.
