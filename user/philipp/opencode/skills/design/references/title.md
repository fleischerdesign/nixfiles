# Title Design

## When to load & job

Use this when words are the event: an opening title, a credit sequence, or animated chapter cards. Build a timed title study that shows how type enters the story, becomes readable, and returns attention to the picture. If the question is which shots tell the story, film and video leads. If the title is driven by live interface state rather than placed on an edit timeline, let motion lead on playback behavior.

## Inputs & concepts

Bring approved wording and billing order where available, font files and their permitted use, footage before and after the sequence, and a versioned cue track. Editorial supplies the cut and its timebase; the producer supplies credit constraints, caption placement, aspect ratios, platform overlays, and allowed picture changes. If wording or picture is provisional, label the stand-ins; exact holds, line breaks, and picture handoffs remain open.

Separate the **title window** (all the frames where the title appears) from the **readable hold** (the span when the full intended text can be read against the moving background). A moving title need not stop to be readable, but an incomplete reveal does not count as a full-line hold. **Billing** governs relationships among names and roles, including order and relative prominence where specified; typographic hierarchy is not a license to change those terms. A **picture handoff** is the point where the audience should look away from the type and back to the scene. An image change can compete with the credit even when they do not overlap geometrically.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Hold or weave.** Give long or unfamiliar names a clearly readable span. A short, familiar title can emerge through movement; it shares attention with the picture for more of its screen time.
- **Separate or embed.** A dedicated card tolerates busy footage and varied crops but interrupts the action. Embed words when a plane or edge in the scene gives them purpose, and allow separate placement for different crops.
- **Land on the cue or let it lead.** Put the readable arrival on an accent for a single event, or bring sound in first for anticipation. If muted playback matters, let the framing and progression convey that arrival without the cue.

1. Make a **title placement and timing board**: include the approved text, picture windows by version, required overlays, and an entry, readable hold, and exit track. Place the longest name and the most demanding script on frames where the background is brightest or busiest, not only on the hero still. For fitting actual strings and preserving script shaping, see [Type setting](knowledge/type-setting.md) (`references/knowledge/type-setting.md`).
2. Work backward from the picture handoff. Sketch where the last complete line can remain visible without stealing the next beat; spend the remaining window on entry and exit. Use [Motion timing](knowledge/motion-timing.md) (`references/knowledge/motion-timing.md`) to express the beats and readable interval, and [Sound cues](knowledge/sound-cues.md) (`references/knowledge/sound-cues.md`) to spot the accent against the chosen picture event.
3. Composite the study with the adjacent shots and export the consequential crop variants. If an overlay collides in the narrow version, recompose the title rather than scaling the whole frame or changing a contracted credit relationship.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A credit sits over a station entrance. A light sweeps across the name just after its final letter arrives, then a face turns to camera. The board reveals that the apparent hold is divided into two short readable fragments. Move the complete name's arrival before the sweep and take the sweep as the exit, so the turn belongs to the face. For a narrow crop where that placement intersects captions, move the credit to another region or propose a separate card. Starting the hold at the first letter makes a late reveal followed by a dissolve look generous on paper while offering almost no complete name on screen.

## Standards, limits & handover

If the broadcaster invokes it, [EBU R 095](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/r095), *Safe areas for 16:9 television production*, is a broadcast recommendation; `verify` revision and applicable graphics area before drawing guides. It is not a universal margin for vertical or platform video. Font shaping behavior depends on the renderer and font; Microsoft's [OpenType 1.9.1 specification](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/opentype/spec/), “OpenType Layout,” documents the layout model, not a reading-duration rule. Use production's crop, overlay, and billing specifications rather than inferred defaults.

Show the study with the preceding and following shots, alongside the timing board and any unresolved placements. Ask whether type should interrupt the story or sit within the scene. The producer supplies outstanding credit copy and billing; editorial supplies the conformed picture and cue positions.
