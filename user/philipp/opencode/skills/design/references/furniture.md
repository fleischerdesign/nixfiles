# Furniture

## When to load & job

Use this for a chair, table, storage piece, or furniture family. Brief the relationship between the body, the piece, and whatever it sits beside. The drawing must explain the intended posture and how somebody gets into, uses, and leaves that position. Interior leads when the room is the main deliverable and the piece is one element in its arrangement. This is a fit and use study for the furniture designer and producer, not a shop drawing.

## Inputs & concepts

Ask users or the operator about task duration, posture changes, aids, clothing, and whether the piece is assigned or shared. Get measured floor area, neighboring fixtures, and obstacles along delivery and storage routes from the site team. Obtain supplier drawings that locate the underside, feet, controls, joints, and hardware projections. If a dimension matters but has not been supplied, name the actual faces and position to measure; an appealing silhouette is not a dimension source.

**Working height** is the height of the task, which can be above the nominal tabletop if a tray or apparatus sits on it. **Usable underside** is the volume between the user's approach and their working position after accounting for aprons, crossbars, cable trays, and brackets. **Seat reference height** must say whether it means the unloaded surface or the surface in use; upholstery and posture affect that relationship. The user who needs room to approach may not be the user with the shortest seated reach. Treat them as separate use cases, not body dimensions to combine into one imaginary person. **Handling footprint** includes the space for hands and staff during moving, not only the footprint where a piece comes to rest.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Adjustment versus multiple fixed sizes.** Shared workstations can offer adjustment where postures differ, at the cost of controls, setup time, and upkeep. Fixed options are simpler if users can choose readily, but consume floor and storage space and may not be available where needed.
- **Continuous seating edge versus freedom under the table.** Perimeter legs can free the middle but crowd corner positions; a central support can help at corners but compete with feet further in. Show intermediate places, not just a symmetrical middle seat.
- **Stacking versus seated form.** Frequent resets favor compact storage, but stacking can constrain the seat and back shape. If stacking is rare, the operator may prefer a different moving or storage approach rather than give up a preferred seated profile.

1. Choose the dominant task and the handling episode most likely to change the piece. Make a **furniture pairing sheet** with one row per seat–surface or storage–route pairing: user, task, working position, obstructing part, and source or requested measurement.
2. For approach, use, and withdrawal in that pairing, apply [Fit envelopes](knowledge/fit-envelopes.md) (`references/knowledge/fit-envelopes.md`) to place the body, furniture, and moving parts in context. Keep each option on the same floor and work-surface references.
3. Show what construction choice changes the conflict. Draw a transverse section through supports as well as a side section where the user pulls in. Put the folded or stacked form next to the usable form; mark the part a mover grasps and where a cart or stored stack is intended to go.
4. Give the producer the pairing sheet and identify what the full-scale mock-up needs to make interchangeable, such as apron position, leg layout, or seat height. The handover should distinguish a posture question from a construction question.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A communal study table will be used with a chair offered in the venue. The advertised heights look compatible, but the active pairing is the occupied seat, the user's thighs, and the first obstruction crossed on the way toward the work. In the side section, the apron interrupts that path before the user can bring their forearms to the page. Moving the apron back could free the entry path but still put it where the user's knees settle; the section therefore marks both the approach and the seated position. The transverse cut then shows the corner seat close to a leg. Relocating supports helps one pairing while creating a question elsewhere; show those changed positions together. Request the underside profile and the occupied seat position before suggesting finished dimensions.

Matching catalog heights alone hides what actually stops the chair or the body. Show the obstructing element in section and ask the specialist to reproduce it in the full-scale pairing, not merely tape out a tabletop.

## Standards, limits & handover

[ISO 7250-1:2017](https://www.iso.org/standard/65246.html) defines anthropometric measurements, not furniture dimensions; `verify` the applicable edition and measurement section. EN 1335-1:2020+A1:2022 covers dimensions and their determination for office work chairs; `verify` the applicable edition, section, and product scope before borrowing any requirement. A client's inventory or supplier drawing is a project input; pairing rows and silhouette overlays are conventions. The study table example specifies no normative dimensions.

Show the concept brief, use and storage views, and the pairings that changed the design. Ask which posture dominates and who moves the piece. A furniture specialist leads the full-scale study with representative users; the producer supplies evidence on stability, loads, joints, and process constraints. Final dimensions, construction details, and fabrication remain outside the study.
