# Lifecycle scope

## Use when

Two options may provide the same service through different products, operating patterns or lifetimes. Produce a comparison statement, side-by-side process boundaries, and a supplier and operations inventory request before making any environmental impact claim.

## Inputs

Find out what decision the comparison informs, its audience, the current baseline, and the conditions of use: service quality, geography, time period and relevant operating patterns. Collect any measured quantities, lifetimes, loss and replacement rates, and routes from the organizations that record them. Note what the data actually represent, including units and place. Without quantities or a defined baseline, draft scope alternatives and data questions rather than defaulting to generic factors.

## Procedure

1. Define the **functional unit** as an amount of service under stated conditions, not a count of the objects sold. Identify the **reference flows** for each option: how much product or other service is needed to deliver that unit. If quality or service coverage differs, describe the difference and agree what comparison is meaningful before normalizing quantities.
2. Diagram both systems for the same functional unit: materials and manufacture, distribution, use, upkeep or return, replacement and end-of-life routes as relevant. Draw processes where the burdens happen, even if someone other than the buyer performs them. A narrow manufacturing boundary can answer a factory question; it cannot stand in for a comparison shaped by reuse and disposal.
3. Attach an **inventory request** to the diagrams. For each consequential process, ask for the amount, unit and denominator, observation period, geography, data source and owner. Request both an operating average and information about variation where it can change the comparison. Mark exclusions, shared processes, allocation questions and gaps with their reasons rather than silently omitting them or entering zero.
4. Expose the variables that could change the comparison, such as realized lifetime, split between disposal routes or incremental rather than shared transport. State what evidence would replace each assumption. Hand the boundary and requests to the operations owners and LCA specialist before moving from inventory to impact modeling.

## Limits

This scoping artifact is not an inventory result, an impact assessment, or an environmental claim. ISO supplies the LCA framework and requirements; the project supplies the functional-unit conditions and operating evidence. A specialist should determine the applicable requirements for the intended audience, particularly for a public comparative assertion. Do not transfer impacts from another geography or product without addressing representativeness.

## Worked example

In an illustrative lunch service, the comparison is packaging for meals delivered intact on the same routes, not one reusable container against one disposable container. The reuse diagram includes containers lost before return, trips to retrieve empties and washing between uses. It asks dispatch for whether pickups add distance or ride on existing routes, the wash operator for utilities per load and actual load sizes, and purchasing for replacement records. The single-use diagram asks for packaging quantities and what local collection actually does with them. The visible difference is which processes and owners enter the comparison, not which option has lower impacts.
