# Option framing

## Use when

A brief names a solution before explaining what should change, or alternatives are being discussed on different terms. Produce a decision brief with an explicit baseline, structurally distinct options, and scenarios that reveal which assumptions could change the preference.

## Inputs

Ask the decision owner who should experience a different outcome, over what horizon, and which resources and commitments are already fixed. Gather the present operating model, changes already expected, and evidence for constraints, costs and effects. Separate facts from forecasts and preferences. If capacity or demand is unknown, show the dependency instead of filling a comparison with estimated values.

## Procedure

1. Rewrite the choice without the requested solution: “How can evening applicants complete submission without waiting for a staffed appointment?” Distinguish the outcome from a lever, and record what is outside the decision. If the levers all depend on changing policy, expose that decision rather than comparing implementation details.
2. Describe the **baseline** for the same period as the options. Include known changes already underway, and name continuing costs or gaps; “continue” is an operating choice, not an empty column. Develop alternatives that differ in mechanism, resource allocation or commitment, not merely branding or feature count. Include a reversible step if it buys meaningful information.
3. Lay out options using the same dimensions: affected people, operating change, likely consequence, work displaced, reversibility and dependencies. Annotate each consequence with its evidence or assumption. Keep real constraints distinct from preferences and leave conflicts in view; do not pool them into one aggregate judgment.
4. Pick uncertainties that could reverse the preference. Write contrasting, internally consistent conditions and walk the same options through them. Show where the preference changes and what observation or decision would resolve that particular uncertainty. When a deadline removes a reversible option, show what committing now preserves and what it puts at risk.

## Limits

The comparison is conditional, not a forecast or a decision in the human's place. Scenario labels and comparison dimensions are working conventions. A funding limit or delivery date is a project specification only when supplied by its owner. Do not present an illustrative scenario as a predicted likelihood.

## Worked example

In an illustrative permits office, “add a chatbot” becomes “help applicants submit complete forms outside staffed hours.” The baseline is the existing form and planned help-text refresh. One option adds a guided asynchronous submission path; another shifts some existing appointments to evenings. The guided path matters if the blockage is understanding what to attach. If most stalled cases need someone to interpret eligibility, evening appointments may resolve more cases while moving capacity away from daytime users. The brief puts recent stalled cases alongside the choice so the team can investigate the distinction rather than debate chatbot features.
