# Research methods

## Use when

A design decision depends on knowing how a task happens, what people understand, or how widespread a condition is. Produce a question-to-evidence plan, a recruitment matrix, a session guide or instrument, and a synthesis record that can be traced back to observations.

## Inputs

Start with the decision and what evidence would change it. Get the population of interest, candidate recruitment channels, existing records and how they were produced, access to real contexts and materials, and the research owner's consent and retention rules. Treat coverage and permissions as open until the owner supplies them. Without participant access, draft the instrument and name which claims remain unsupported.

## Procedure

1. Write each question with its intended inference. To learn what work actually happens, observe a real or recently completed task and ask about decisions in context. To learn how people interpret a prototype, give them a goal without prescribing its controls. To estimate prevalence, design a survey around a defined population and a defensible sampling approach. Interviews can expose mechanisms, not estimate population prevalence.
2. Make a **recruitment matrix** crossing decision-relevant circumstances with contact routes. List who each route misses, such as people who stopped before creating an account. Recruit for useful contrasts in qualitative work; for a survey, plan coverage, nonresponse and mode effects with the research lead. Offer participation modes or accommodations suited to the population rather than filtering out people who need them.
3. Draft the **session guide** around a concrete episode, with neutral prompts and room for interruptions or workarounds. Separate observed action, participant account, moderator intervention and interpretation in the note schema. For surveys, use a bounded recall period, wording that asks about one thing at a time, and response choices that fit the question. Try out the wording and flow before fieldwork.
4. Record an **evidence chain**: anonymized source, observation, plausible interpretation, contradicting case and design implication. Keep rival explanations visible. If a short study cannot reach a consequential group or setting, narrow the conclusion to the conditions seen and identify what the remaining study should seek.

## Limits

Research instruments and sampling details are project specifications; the methods below are not norms. Self-report, field observation and prototype behavior answer different questions. A convenient contact list does not become a representative sample by growing larger. Do not infer motives or publish identifiable material from notes unless the evidence and consent support that use.

## Worked example

In an illustrative appointment study, the team asks whether to add another reminder or show preparation instructions earlier. It observes people reconstructing their last booking using the messages they received and recruits both attendees and people who rescheduled through phone support. One participant opens the reminder promptly but only discovers a document requirement after following its link; another already has the document. The synthesis separates “reminder seen” from “requirements found in time” and links that distinction to showing the requirement in the initial confirmation. It does not infer how many missed visits had that cause.
