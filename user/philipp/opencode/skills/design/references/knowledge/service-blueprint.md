# Service blueprint

## Use when

A user episode spans channels, staff roles, systems or shifts, and the experience depends on work the user cannot see. Produce a layered blueprint and an exception branch detailed enough for staff to rehearse a handoff without guessing who owns the case.

## Inputs

Bring a recent case or observation, user messages or receipts, front-line and operations accounts, and the records or queues that move work. Ask service owners who can make the next decision, what information may move, and what timing commitments are actually made. Mark each step as observed, reported or proposed. When queue timing or authority is unknown, draft the branch with its open condition rather than inheriting a promise from the interface copy.

## Procedure

1. Give the episode a trigger and an end recognizable by the user. Lay out user actions and visible evidence, such as a receipt or status message, in columns ordered by event. Include gaps where the user's wait spans multiple internal steps.
2. Under each episode, show frontstage actions, backstage work and supporting processes. Draw the line of visibility between work the user experiences and work they do not. Connect dependencies with arrows only where one event initiates or enables another; line up asynchronous work with its own start and completion events.
3. For each transfer, name the sending role, the receiving role or queue, the case reference, minimum context, pending decision and next user-visible update. Distinguish assignment from acceptance: name who retains responsibility between them and what constitutes receipt. A queue may hold the record without being the role accountable for it.
4. Branch from an actual exception trigger: no receipt, missing information, duplicate case, rejected transfer, or delayed external response. Show who acts, where the case returns, what the user hears, and how the branch re-enters or ends. If the receiving team is not staffed continuously, map custody between shifts rather than drawing a synchronous handoff. Rehearse with the relevant roles and annotate disputed steps beside the branch.

## Limits

A blueprint represents a particular episode and operating assumption, not measured waiting times or a staffing model. The lanes are conventions. Data-sharing permissions and response commitments come from the service owner and applicable rules; proposed routing does not itself authorize disclosure or guarantee capacity.

## Worked example

An illustrative repair service sends a booking request from chat to a dispatch queue. The user sees “Request received,” not “Visit booked.” The blueprint keeps the chat team responsible for updates until dispatch accepts the case, carrying the case reference, availability and unresolved scheduling need. In the no-receipt branch the chat team consults the existing record and follows up with dispatch rather than opening a second request. When dispatch accepts, it becomes responsible for the booking update. The user gets a separate message only once a slot exists. The blueprint shows why rewriting the first confirmation cannot fix the ownership gap.
