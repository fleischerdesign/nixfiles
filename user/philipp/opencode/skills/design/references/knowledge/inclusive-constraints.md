# Inclusive constraints

## Use when

A task quietly assumes one way to perceive information, act, communicate, or recover. Produce a task-and-barrier record, a requirement for the affected step, and a comparison between ways of reaching the same outcome. The method applies to a physical route or staffed service as much as an interface.

## Inputs

Bring actual task sequences, error and recovery paths, environment and channel limits, and accounts from people who experience exclusion. Ask participants how they prefer to take part and what support, language, equipment, or timing affects participation. Separate observations from scenarios the team has imagined. Without participant evidence, label proposed barriers and routes as hypotheses and arrange participation rather than assigning needs to presumed personas.

## Procedure

1. Describe the mismatch at a specific step: task, conditions, ability or resource the design assumes, and what the person loses. Consider permanent, temporary and situational constraints as prompts, not as interchangeable experiences. A loud room and hearing loss may both expose reliance on audio, while suggesting different recovery needs.
2. Write the requirement in task language: “While waiting, the visitor can tell whether it is their turn without relying on hearing the announcement.” Preserve the information needed for the next step and record consequences such as lost place, involuntary disclosure or the need to ask another person. Leave technical values to the project specification or applicable norm.
3. First explore a shared path that removes the dependency. When a distinct path is needed, draw both from discovery through recovery. Compare access hours, cost, privacy, effort, independence, predictability and the outcome. If the alternative requires staff, name the staffing dependency and show what happens when someone is unavailable or the person prefers not to disclose why they need help.
4. Bring these episodes to people who encounter the conditions and to operators of both paths. Offer meaningful options for participating in review, including ways to give feedback without reenacting a harmful episode. Record how accounts differ, which constraints can be removed at the source, and which alternatives still carry an extra burden.

## Limits

The Persona Spectrum and universal design principles are design guidance, not substitutes for participant evidence or applicable technical norms. An equivalent outcome on paper does not establish equivalent access in practice. Local requirements and implementation values come from the relevant primary norm, project owner, or supplier.

## Worked example

In an illustrative walk-in clinic, a spoken name can be missed and can also disclose identity in the room. The requirement is to recognize one's turn and know where to go without hearing or publicly revealing a name. A study pairs a visible ticket call with a spoken ticket call and destination. It also sketches an opt-in direct cue for people who cannot use the shared announcements; the front desk retains the person's queue position if the cue is missed. The study contrasts this with “ask the desk for help,” which leaves discovery, privacy and recovery to the visitor.
