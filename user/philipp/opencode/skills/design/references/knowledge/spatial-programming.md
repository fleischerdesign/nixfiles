# Spatial programming

## Use when

An operating brief needs to become a schedule of spaces and a choice between layouts. Produce an activity register, a space program, an adjacency matrix, and paired plan and section studies. Defer room shapes until you know which activities share resources and which need separation.

## Inputs

Gather operating episodes, people and equipment present together, setup and reset, storage and deliveries, occupied hours, and noise or privacy needs from operators. Bring the survey, site access and levels, and any area or budget brief from their owners. Record headcounts as simultaneous or throughput, and areas with units and measurement basis. An unknown peak or survey dimension stays open; you can still sketch relationships without implying that the program fits.

## Procedure

1. Make an **activity register**: actor, task, concurrent people, equipment, time window, and preceding and following tasks. Include cleaning, shift changes and replenishment. Distinguish simultaneous uses from uses that can share a room if setup, storage, scheduling, and independent access support it.
2. Translate clusters into **space program** rows. Give each a stable ID, room count, capacity basis, area per room or provisional range, and conditions such as daylight or secured access. Derive tentative area from furniture, working positions and movement, not room names. Summarize net programmed area separately from circulation, partitions and building services; state what each subtotal includes and leave unknown allowances visible.
3. Put space IDs on both axes of an **adjacency matrix**. Use a descriptive legend: direct connection, nearby, separate, no relationship, and unknown. Attach a reason and the flow involved to consequential pairs. If a route is directional or only needed at certain hours, annotate the direction and time instead of treating the matrix as fully symmetric.
4. Draw contrasting block plans against the same site assumptions. Overlay actual routes, including crossings, and cut sections through the busiest level change and hardest spatial dependency. Compare the same trips across variants, identifying what each sacrifices. Under a tight footprint, stack a cluster or time-share a room and redraw the routes or the timetable; do not silently shrink the program.

## Limits

Matrix labels are design conventions, not code categories. A client area cap is a project specification. A plan and section study is not a surveyed fit, a structural or services design, or a code analysis; the project team needs measured conditions and applicable requirements before using it as a dimensional basis.

## Worked example

In an illustrative library, evening classes need a workshop and toilet while the collection is locked. The program separates workshop cleanup from public handwashing. The matrix calls for direct workshop-to-cleanup access and an after-hours entry-to-toilet route outside the collection boundary. A spread plan groups those uses at the entrance but takes frontage from the collection. A stacked plan frees frontage; its section reveals that an upstairs workshop would depend on a vertical route behind the locked line. Moving that route outside the line changes the plan more fundamentally than shifting the workshop door.
