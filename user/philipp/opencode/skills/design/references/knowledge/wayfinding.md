# Map decisions, reassurance and recovery on a route

## Use when

People must reach a destination without knowing the site. Produce a destination vocabulary, a directed decision-point map and an approach-by-approach cue sequence that includes a missed turn.

## Inputs

Get a current site plan, arrival modes and entrances, destination lists, actual words from tickets or booking messages, and routes available by time and user needs. Ask the operator about closures, locked doors, lifts, staff coverage and recurring confusion. Gather approach photos and actual sightlines where possible. Mark unobserved links on the route map instead of treating a line on the plan as usable.

## Procedure

1. **Make a naming table.** Give each destination an ID, public name, official name if different, translations and any zone aliases. Record where visitors first encounter the name. Use one public label on booking, signs, maps and staff prompts. If grouping is necessary, introduce the association before asking anyone to choose a zone, for example “Blood Tests · East Wing.”
2. **Draw the journeys as directed paths.** Start at each real arrival, follow permissible links, and mark choices at forks, level changes, controlled doors or entrances to a decision area. Record the approach direction and what the traveler can see. The same junction approached from the opposite side may need different directions. Keep time-dependent or step-free routes explicit where they diverge rather than adding an unexplained arrow to the main route.
3. **Write the cue sequence.** For each choice, put the direction where a person can still take it. At the junction show only destinations or groups that distinguish the choices; after a turn or level change, repeat enough of the vocabulary to reassure the traveler. Confirm arrival with a destination identifier. Draw the views in order, including what sits near the proposed signs, so a sign that is present on a plan is not mistaken for one visible before the decision.
4. **Draw the likely wrong branch.** Identify the next recognizable landmark or junction after a missed turn. Give the visitor a cue toward a known node or a usable alternate path. Document cue location, facing, exact message, sign function, and operating exceptions on the sign schedule. Use the same words in the recovery cue rather than introducing a second naming system.

## Limits

The map is a design proposal, not evidence of route usability, visibility in a crowd or site conditions. The operator and site specialists can use a walk-through and temporary signs with representative visitors to review the sequence. For U.S. projects, establish which signs are scoped by §216 before applying §703 technical provisions; route and egress obligations are separate.

## Worked example

In an illustrative clinic, booking says “Blood Tests,” yet the corridor directory groups rooms under “Diagnostics.” At the lobby use “Blood Tests · East Wing,” then give “Blood Tests” at the fork where the east corridor branches. Repeat the name after the turn and at the door. On the straight-ahead path, the next public junction points back toward “Blood Tests” via the signed fork, before a visitor reaches the staff-only door. The sequence uses the appointment wording without listing every clinic at every junction.
