# Advertising

## When to load & job

Use this for a campaign premise and the ads that carry it across placements. Decide what someone should remember or do after encountering an ad, and make the route from that encounter to the offer visible. Brand design supplies the identity; advertising gives that identity something specific to say now. Deliver a copy and placement study if the offer or production inputs are unresolved, or artwork for the specified placements when those inputs are available.

## Inputs & concepts

Get the audience's starting knowledge and campaign objective from the brief; get placement dimensions, viewing conditions, interaction options, and platform overlays from the media team. Ask the offer owner for claim wording, evidence and its scope, terms, and the actual destination. Get current identity assets and partner rules from the brand owner. If an input is missing, label the assumed placement and use supplied facts only; leave unsupported copy and final exports out.

The **premise** is the idea every placement expresses, not the headline every placement repeats. The **offer** is what someone can actually obtain; the **claim** is what the ad explicitly or implicitly says about it. A **qualifier** constrains the claim, for example by naming who is eligible. The **action** is what the placement lets someone do and the **destination** is what they reach by doing it. Treat those as separate fields: “Book an assessment” is misleading if the destination only accepts expressions of interest.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Lead with the situation or the difference.** For people new to the category, show a familiar situation and what the offer changes. That costs space before the pitch. For people already comparing options, lead with a documented difference; it gets to the point but assumes they know why the category matters.
- **Hold the premise steady, not the copy stack.** A glance-only poster needs one focal point and the brand close to it; a high-intent placement can foreground the offer and next action. Shared cues tie the ads together, even when their reading order changes.
- **Scope the claim to the evidence.** Put a meaning-changing condition next to the claim. If they cannot travel together, narrow or drop the claim instead of asking a footnote to reverse a stronger headline. This may cost punch but avoids promising a different offer.

1. Write two one-sentence premises before writing headlines: one starts with the audience's situation, the other with the offer's supported difference. Pick the premise that serves the campaign objective and describe what would make the other one more useful.
2. Build a **placement-and-claim matrix**. Give each row an encounter, a first takeaway, the proposed words or implied claim, its evidence and condition, the next action, and the actual destination. Read each row as a journey: what will people expect to see or receive when they act?
3. Block placement flats from the matrix, using real copy and an adjacent view of the destination. Where campaign artwork is in scope, use [Image selection](knowledge/image-selection.md) (`references/knowledge/image-selection.md`) when a frame must support a story across crops, and [Identity systems](knowledge/identity-systems.md) (`references/knowledge/identity-systems.md`) when recognition or partner attribution must survive layout changes. If that artwork also goes to a printer, use [Print PDF](knowledge/print-pdf.md) (`references/knowledge/print-pdf.md`) to translate the supplier's geometry and export requirements.
4. Assemble each placement as delivered. Where the platform supplies a button or caption outside the asset, separate that field from the artwork and show both together in the placement preview. Record the destination alongside that exact variant so the media team can wire the action without inferring it from the headline.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

In an illustrative bike-repair campaign, the offer sheet says a safety assessment is free on Tuesdays; repairs cost extra. The destination shows hours and location but takes no bookings. The high-intent feed row can lead with “Free Tuesday bike safety assessment” and say “Repairs priced separately” alongside it. Its action is “See hours and location,” not “Book now.” For a glance-only station poster, propose “Bike safety assessments on Tuesdays,” paired with the actual short destination supplied by the campaign owner. Removing “free” gives up a price hook where the distinction between the assessment and repairs may not fit. Both ads can use the same mechanic-and-bike image without implying that repair labor is included.

Shrinking the social layout for the poster preserves the button graphic but loses the click. “Learn more” becomes a dead end. Give the poster a destination legible from the intended viewing distance, and keep the clickable action in the feed.

## Standards, limits & handover

For U.S. advertising, consult the FTC's [Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation) (1984), on prior substantiation of objective claims, and [.com Disclosures](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/com-disclosures-how-make-effective-disclosures-digital-advertising) (March 2013), on proximity and prominence in digital placements (`verify` current guidance and applicability with the claims owner). These are regulatory policy and guidance, not layout presets. For printed artwork, Adobe's [InDesign PDF export options](https://helpx.adobe.com/indesign/desktop/save-export-and-publish/save-and-export/adobe-pdf-export-options.html), “Marks and Bleeds” and “Output,” describe the controls (`verify` installed version); the printer supplies the job values. One focal point is a design convention, not a placement specification.

Show the premise, two ads in their viewing contexts, the destination each points to, and the corresponding rows of evidence and conditions. The claims owner handles substantiation and applicable rules; the media or print supplier owns delivery specs. Ask which placement introduces the offer versus captures demand, and which offer the destination will actually honor.
