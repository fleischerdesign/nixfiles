# Service Design

## When to load & job

Load this for a journey, service blueprint, or rehearsal brief that crosses channels, roles, shifts, or providers. The journey shows the episode as the person experiences it; the blueprint explains what work must happen to produce that experience. Use interaction design instead if the question stays inside one interface and does not depend on an operating handoff. The artifact proposes or documents a bounded episode for review. A prototype or rehearsal can make a promise tangible, but cannot establish live capacity, wait times, or resolution rates.

## Inputs & concepts

Gather touchpoint messages, forms, receipts and branch scripts from channel owners; case histories and queue rules from operations; and service policy, commitments and data permissions from their owners. Ask staff for the work they do outside the prescribed flow, including how they find a case when someone switches channels. Seek the person's account of what they were trying to accomplish, including what they tried before entering the visible workflow. Where records are absent, call the episode hypothetical or proposed and leave timing, frequency and exception handling open.

A **touchpoint** is a moment of contact, not a team or channel in the abstract. **Service evidence** is what a person can use to understand where they stand: a receipt, a call, a status, or silence following a promise. **Frontstage** work is visible to the person; **backstage** work and supporting processes make it possible. A **case** persists across contacts even when each channel records a separate interaction. Distinguish custody of the record, responsibility for the next action, and authority to decide an exception. A queue can provide the first without settling either of the others.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Whole goal versus close-up.** Use the person's goal as the boundary when the problem crosses channels. Zoom in when one transfer needs redesign, but carry forward earlier attempts and outstanding commitments so the close-up does not start with a blank case.
- **Warm transfer versus asynchronous queue.** Live coordination can preserve context and confirm acceptance but requires staff availability on both sides. A queue can absorb uneven demand across shifts, but the receiving team may not see the case in time to honor the sending team's promise.
- **Clickable prototype versus staff rehearsal.** Use screens to surface what people infer from wording and status. Rehearse when delivery depends on coordination, permissions, or exception authority; a screen cannot tell you whether an off-shift team will respond.

1. Scope one episode by its trigger, actor, conditions and recognizable resolution. Create an **episode evidence ledger**: for each consequential moment, record what the person knew, what operations knew, and the source or gap on each side. Keep recorded behavior, staff accounts and proposed changes distinct.
2. When the ledger leaves a consequential gap, load [Research methods](knowledge/research-methods.md) (`references/knowledge/research-methods.md`) to frame evidence requests or participant sessions suited to that gap. For instance, ask whether a person can distinguish receipt from resolution, rather than assuming the existing confirmation communicates it.
3. Use the ledger to draw the current journey if evidence supports one; mark proposed changes separately so the reviewer can tell which operating commitments are new. Load [Service blueprint](knowledge/service-blueprint.md) (`references/knowledge/service-blueprint.md`) to connect visible episodes to backstage work, custody and exception branches. Put each proposed promise next to the event that would make it true.
4. Select a slice that exposes the hardest transfer. When drafting the handoff message, use [UX writing](knowledge/ux-writing.md) (`references/knowledge/ux-writing.md`) to say what is known, what context carries forward and who acts next. Stage it using the proposed message or script, a representative dummy case record, and role prompts. Record disagreements about workload, authority and context as open operating decisions on the blueprint, not as edits to copy alone.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

In an illustrative refund service, the first receipt says the request was submitted. Operations can see that a refund instruction was sent downstream, but not whether the money reached the customer. The evidence ledger shows that “sent” belongs to the operator's side and does not substantiate “refunded” on the customer's side. The proposed blueprint keeps the case reference usable after submission and adds a delayed-payment episode: support retrieves the existing case, explains the last known state, and routes investigation to the role that can obtain settlement information. In rehearsal, start with a customer asking about a missing payment after a shift change, not with the initial form.

Ending the map at submission leads to a reassuring confirmation and no means of resolving a stalled payment. Extend the map to the person's end state and state where reliable confirmation of that state would come from.

## Standards, limits & handover

G. Lynn Shostack, “Designing Services That Deliver” (1984), and Mary Jo Bitner, Amy L. Ostrom and Felicia N. Morgan, “Service Blueprinting: A Practical Technique for Service Innovation,” *California Management Review* 50(3), 2008, pp. 66–94, are published methods, not service norms. Lanes and the ledger are working conventions. Timing promises and authorized transfer fields are project specifications; applicable privacy or sector obligations are normative only within their scope (`verify` jurisdiction, applicability and section with the responsible specialist). The refund states are example values. Use a shared diagram and dummy case record for rehearsal; keep identifiable case evidence in its authorized system.

Show the episode, blueprint and proposed messages together, with simulated steps marked. Service staff can explain queue and shift practice; researchers can study interpretation; operations needs real case data for actual waits and resolutions. Ask who may accept an exception and what information may move without making the person retell the case.
