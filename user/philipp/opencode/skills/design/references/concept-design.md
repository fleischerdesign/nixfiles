# Concept Design

## When to load & job

Use this for sketches, scenario boards, and visual development that help a team decide what to make before it commits to production. A concept should make a proposed object, setting, or behavior tangible enough to discuss. It does not settle engineering, construction, or final asset detail.

Lead here when the premise is still changing. Use 3D when the primary deliverable is the scene or asset itself, and the relevant product or spatial discipline when the team needs to resolve real fit, construction or operation. A concept board or proxy scene is a study for those makers, not their specification.

## Inputs & concepts

Get the premise, people or characters, point of view, intended use and story beats from the brief owner. Ask the production lead which dimensions, interfaces or movement constraints are supplied and which are provisional. Without measurements, draw relative scale with a labeled person or known object and leave fitted geometry to the relevant specialist.

A **silhouette** isolates a form's outer profile at a given view; it answers questions about recognition but can hide an opening or overlap. A **beat** is a change that matters to the viewer or user, such as spotting an entrance or reaching an operating position. **Blocking** places bodies and objects through those beats before investing in detail. A **movement envelope** is the region occupied over a motion, not simply the shape at either endpoint. A rough envelope makes a conflict discussable; it does not establish actual clearance.

## Decisions & procedure

- Compare silhouettes when recognition at first exposure is uncertain; you lose interior detail but can see whether the alternatives differ beyond ornament. If interaction matters more, draw approach, use and departure; trade polish for visible change over time.
- Use a cutaway or section when the exterior and the internal arrangement depend on each other. A surface study can speak more directly to atmosphere and material, but does not answer a question about volume or movement.

1. Describe the unresolved choice as a question about the proposed experience. Define one moment and viewpoint common to all variants. Use [Option framing](knowledge/option-framing.md) (`references/knowledge/option-framing.md`) when the brief already prescribes a form and you need to recover the outcome it was meant to serve.
2. Draw two roughs that differ in organization, not ornament. On a **beat-and-view board**, give each rough the same scale anchor, relevant viewpoints and scenario beats. Mark geometry supplied by the project differently from invented or provisional geometry.
3. Develop the view that exposes the key relationship. Keep the camera or section cut fixed across alternatives if the question concerns a change in form. For building proxy geometry or reasoning about projection and occlusion, use [Scene studies](knowledge/scene-studies.md) (`references/knowledge/scene-studies.md`).
4. Carry the chosen concept through a short scenario. Annotate why a form or arrangement matters to each beat and where a later maker may change it. Put atmospheric painting on its own panel if it would hide an unresolved spatial relationship.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

In an observatory set concept, the operator must arrive at the eyepiece while the telescope rotates. The first rough reserves a path beside the telescope because there is floor space in the parked view. On the beat-and-view board, draw the arrival route in plan and use the same section cut for two positions with the operator in place. Add an outline for the part of the swing that intersects the route: two clean poses can still conceal a crossing in between. In a second rough, move the approach to the other side of the set or delay the operator's entry until the telescope has turned. That changes the visible beat, so show the new arrival as well as the geometry. The shapes and timing are example assumptions, not measured positions.

A finished interior painting can make the telescope feel plausible without ever showing where the operator stands. Keep that painting for the light and atmosphere question; draw the route and motion as geometry before making it more beautiful.

## Standards, limits & handover

Scott Robertson with Thomas Bertling, *How to Draw* (2013), the section on perspective terminology, is a drawing method, not a requirement for a particular projection; `verify` the section title in the edition used. The Blender 4.5 LTS Manual, “[Cameras](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.5/render/cameras.html),” documents perspective and orthographic camera types for proxy views. The common scale figure and beat board are working conventions. Actual geometry, camera access and movement limits are project or production inputs, not values inferred from the drawings.

Show competing roughs, the chosen scenario and annotated views that separate intent from assumptions. Ask which beat must stay clear if the form changes. For a set, the production designer or technical lead develops a dimensioned layout and addresses movement and construction before the study is used to make the set.
