# Social Design

## When to load & job

Use this to brief an intervention that changes how people share resources, make decisions, or carry responsibility. A service blueprint shows how delivery works; a social design brief asks who gets to shape it, who benefits, and who absorbs the costs. Make the authority and proposed distribution of work visible for discussion. The deliverable is a participation and intervention brief, not community agreement, a validated strategy, or measured social impact.

## Inputs & concepts

Get the sponsor’s mandate, budget owner, limits on delegation, existing community-led arrangements, and any documented accounts from affected people. Ask local organizers about informal work already keeping the arrangement functioning, and who relies on it without appearing on a membership or customer list. Record whose account you have, whether it can be shared, and what it cannot tell you. A sponsor-only account leaves people’s priorities and willingness to participate open.

Distinguish **formal authority** (who can sign, allocate money, or change a rule) from **practical power** (who can withhold space, time, information, or labor). An open invitation changes who may speak, not who decides. A **distributional consequence** is a gain or burden for a particular group, including displaced unpaid work, exposure to retaliation, or lost access. Map groups by their relationship to the decision, not as internally uniform constituencies.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Consultation or shared authority.** If a decision is constrained, consult on named choices and disclose what cannot move. Propose co-design only where the sponsor can share a real decision and fund involvement. It takes more time and can yield an outcome the sponsor would not choose.
- **Separate listening or joint negotiation.** Separate sessions reduce exposure when landlords, managers, or providers can penalize candor, but groups cannot negotiate directly. A joint session can serve an actual negotiation if an independent facilitator and participants can establish terms.
- **Narrow responsibility or broader coverage.** A narrow intervention may keep duties assignable but leave needs unmet; a broader one may depend on unpaid work or fragile cooperation. Expose those dependencies instead of treating reach as an unqualified gain.

1. Write the decision in terms of the rule, resource, or responsibility that can change. Mark decisions outside the sponsor’s authority and identify who could reopen them.
2. Build a **power-and-burden map** with a row for each affected group or role. Record who can propose, decide, object, and hear an appeal; separate these rights from actual ability to use them. Add a row for work with no acknowledged owner. Use [Option framing](knowledge/option-framing.md) (`references/knowledge/option-framing.md`) to compare genuinely different interventions with the current arrangement. On the map, locate the gains and work each would redistribute, including if uptake is low or an existing support network withdraws.
3. Convert sensitive rows into a participation brief: invitation routes, compensated time, language or access support, independence from the sponsor, and who receives individual versus aggregated accounts. Use [Inclusive constraints](knowledge/inclusive-constraints.md) (`references/knowledge/inclusive-constraints.md`) when participation itself excludes affected people, and [Research methods](knowledge/research-methods.md) (`references/knowledge/research-methods.md`) to plan how a local team can hear from people missing from the sponsor’s evidence.
4. Make the return path explicit. State who hears objections, what authority they have, and how participants learn what changed and why. Record unresolved interests rather than compressing disagreement into a single community position.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A housing provider proposes resident-managed bookings for a shared courtyard. In the draft map, residents can schedule events, but caretakers clean up and reconcile complaints while the provider alone can change opening hours. The workshop therefore cannot promise shared control over hours. Compare resident-managed scheduling with provider-run booking plus protected unbooked time. Under the first proposal, make cleaning and dispute handling explicit funded roles or leave them as unresolved work; do not assume a committee will absorb them. Ask a facilitator to hear renters and caretakers separately and take non-identifying objections to the person who can change the terms.

Recruiting only through the neighborhood association makes property owners’ availability look like neighborhood agreement. Add routes not controlled by the association, and show whose voices are still missing when the options are discussed.

## Standards, limits & handover

Sherry R. Arnstein, [“A Ladder of Citizen Participation”](https://doi.org/10.1080/01944366908977225), *Journal of the American Institute of Planners* 35(4), 1969, distinguishes consultation from delegated power. Use it as an analytical lens, not a grading scale. The map is a working convention; delegated authority, confidentiality terms, compensation and redress routes are project terms their owners must supply. `verify` any applicable statutory consultation or privacy duties with the local lead before promising a process.

Show the human the map beside the options and draft invitation, with benefits and burdens labeled as hypotheses. A local facilitator and affected people must surface missing perspectives and decide whether the terms are acceptable; implementation outcomes require follow-up evidence. What can participants actually change? Who can fund their time and act on objections?
