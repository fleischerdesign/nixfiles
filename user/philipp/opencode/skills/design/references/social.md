# Social Design

## When to load & job

Use this for a brief about an intervention that changes how people share resources, make decisions, or carry responsibility. A service blueprint shows how delivery works; this brief asks who gets to shape it, who benefits, and who absorbs the costs.

## Inputs

Start with the sponsor's mandate, decision rights, existing community-led efforts, and any documented accounts from affected people. Map people who aren't customers or formal partners but may still be affected. If you only have the sponsor's account, label the stakeholder map provisional and leave people's priorities and willingness to participate open.

## Decisions & process

- If the decision is already constrained, use consultation on named choices and say what cannot change. If the sponsor can share authority, propose co-design with explicit decision rights and resources for participation. That takes longer and can produce a direction the sponsor wouldn't have chosen. Show who can propose, decide, object, and appeal.
- Choose separate listening sessions when speaking in front of landlords, managers, or providers could carry consequences. A joint session can help groups negotiate a shared problem, but only if an independent facilitator and participants can establish terms for it. Show how disagreement travels from each format to the decision-maker.
- Sketch who gains and who takes on work under each option, including what happens when uptake is low or an existing support network withdraws. A narrower proposal may keep responsibilities clear but leave some needs unmet.

## Typical mistakes & examples

Recruiting only through a neighborhood association can make property owners' availability look like neighborhood agreement. For a shared courtyard brief, plan separate ways for renters and caretakers to contribute, and show who would take on cleaning and dispute resolution under each option.

## Deliverables & questions for the human

Show the power map, participation brief, and distribution of likely benefits and burdens as hypotheses. A local facilitator and affected people must surface missing perspectives and decide whether the participation terms are acceptable; implementation outcomes require follow-up evidence. What decision can participants actually change? Who can fund their time and act on objections?
