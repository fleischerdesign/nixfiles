# 3D

## When to load & job

Use this for a code-based scene, object study, or interactive spatial prototype. Decide what geometry, camera, light, and material must communicate about form or behavior. Let VFX lead when the unresolved question is integration into filmed footage, and let physical product design lead when the decision is about fit or fabrication. A renderable study is useful evidence about the chosen views; it is not a production asset or a substitute for a final render pipeline.

## Inputs & concepts

Bring reference views, known dimensions and units, target framing or interaction, and supplied assets. Get formats, renderer constraints, and expected camera distances from the runtime or production team. Ask the object owner where dimensions are binding and where silhouette can change. Without dimensions, use a labeled relative scale and proxy geometry; fit and exact material appearance remain unresolved.

The **silhouette** is the visible profile at a particular viewpoint. Surface shading does not change that outline: a normal map tilts the shading normals without moving vertices. Camera **projection** determines how depth affects size; an orthographic view keeps equal sizes equal at different depths, whereas perspective makes nearer elements appear larger. **Roughness** controls how broadly a material spreads specular reflections in a typical physically based shader. The reflection's shape also depends on the geometry and what the surface reflects; material settings alone cannot put an edge highlight in the frame.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Model the silhouette or suggest the surface.** Spend geometry on profiles, openings, and edges that must hold up through an orbit or cast real shadows. Use textures or normal maps for fine relief at the intended distance; the detail will not change a grazing silhouette.
- **Explain shape or stage depth.** Use orthographic views for comparing proportions and perspective for the viewer's actual overlap and depth cues. The latter can exaggerate near parts; pair the views before interpreting scale.
- **Show form or show finish.** Start with neutral clay and a broad side light when volume is uncertain. Introduce finish once the shape reads, and give reflective surfaces something to reflect. A dramatic key can sell a finish in one view while hiding the profile in another.

1. Decide which viewing decision the study must support: recognizing a profile at distance, seeing how a part opens, or judging the finish in a close view. Block out the volumes with consistent units and a stable origin. For choosing proxy geometry and comparable projections, use [Scene studies](knowledge/scene-studies.md) (`references/knowledge/scene-studies.md`).
2. Build a **form-and-finish contact sheet**: rows for the intended view and the angle where the form is most ambiguous, columns for clay and proposed finish. Render paired columns with the same camera and geometry. Keep the first finish study under known lighting, so the viewer can attribute changes to finish rather than to a different key.
3. Refine geometry where the camera sees a broken outline, inadequate contact, or a missing edge highlight. For finish, record which maps encode color and which carry data such as roughness or normals. Use [Color management](knowledge/color-management.md) (`references/knowledge/color-management.md`) when textures, renderer output, and review displays need an explicit color route. Present an orbit or second view if the object is meant to be viewed interactively.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A glossy housing's chamfer disappears at the review distance. In the contact sheet, use the clay view to locate the edge, then hold the finish camera and geometry fixed and move a broad reflection across the bevel. If the highlight becomes readable, the initial setup reflected nothing useful there. If it still reduces to a thin flicker at the intended scale, compare a larger bevel as a labeled departure from the supplied dimensions. Turning up the key alone brightens the face, making the edge harder to distinguish rather than putting a reflection where it is needed.

## Standards, limits & handover

For interchange, Khronos [glTF 2.0](https://registry.khronos.org/glTF/specs/2.0/glTF-2.0.html), §3.4, specifies right-handed, Y-up coordinates and meters for linear distances in the format, not necessarily in the source scene. Its “Metallic-Roughness Material” section distinguishes base-color encoding from roughness data. For authoring controls see the Blender 4.5 LTS Manual, [Cameras](https://docs.blender.org/manual/en/4.5/render/cameras.html). `verify` exporter and receiving runtime behavior for the versions in use.

Show the working scene at the intended camera, contact sheet, and orbit or second view. Ask which surfaces must hold up close. A 3D artist needs confirmed dimensions, material references, and the target renderer to build the next asset study; the prototype cannot establish manufactured fit or appearance.
