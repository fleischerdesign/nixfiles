# Sustainable Design

## When to load & job

Use this to scope a comparison of design options with different environmental implications. The deliverable is a comparison brief with process diagrams, operating assumptions, and an inventory request that a life-cycle assessment (LCA) specialist can use. A physical-product study can show how a part is replaced; this brief asks what replacing it changes across the system that provides the service. Material selection, a reuse label, or a diagram does not establish lower impact.

## Inputs & concepts

Get the comparison’s intended use and audience from the decision owner. Ask operators what service they actually deliver, over what period and geography, how the current system runs, and what changes are already committed. Ask suppliers for process and material data, operators for throughput and upkeep records, and downstream handlers for the routes actually used. Preserving the service may require retaining characteristics such as hygiene, reliability, or delivery reach. If the options provide different service, surface that difference instead of hiding it through normalization.

The **functional unit** states the amount of service compared under specified conditions; **reference flows** express what each system needs to supply it. The **system boundary** identifies which processes the comparison represents. An **inventory** describes inputs and outputs; an impact assessment interprets them in impact categories using a specified method. Fuel use or discarded material is not interchangeable with modeled climate change, water scarcity, or another impact category. Keep operating records distinct from assumptions and environmental interpretations.

## Decisions & procedure

- **Per item or per service.** Items are natural for procurement planning; they conceal differences in realized use. Compare per service for redesign decisions and preserve the item counts as operating quantities.
- **Narrow or expanded boundary.** A factory-only view can isolate a supplier decision; extending it to use, return, and disposal exposes work shifted across organizations. The broader question demands evidence from operators the buyer may not control.
- **Single operating case or contrasting cases.** A well-described typical operation is easier to explain. Show contrasting conditions when timing, service volume, or collection patterns could change the comparison. Keep environmental categories separate even when the trade-off leaves no obvious preference.

1. Put the decision and intended audience at the top of a **scope sheet**. Use [Lifecycle scope](knowledge/lifecycle-scope.md) (`references/knowledge/lifecycle-scope.md`) to derive the working comparison basis, boundary diagrams, and inventory requests. Distinguish an unresolved process from one deliberately outside the question; neither warrants a fabricated zero.
2. Annotate the diagrams where an option changes work at another operator, place, or time. Beside each transfer, note who controls it and who records it. Where processes serve multiple products or share resources, distinguish measured totals from proposed ways of attributing them. Leave allocation choices to the specialist.
3. If the intervention changes future operating conditions, use [Feedback loops](knowledge/feedback-loops.md) (`references/knowledge/feedback-loops.md`) to describe delayed effects or competing responses. Attach a short scenario note to the relevant process rather than folding a causal hypothesis into the inventory as a measured quantity.
4. Turn the unresolved differences into an owner-specific data brief. A spreadsheet can keep reported quantities, scenario assumptions, and unknowns in separate fields. Tell the human which broader question cannot be answered by the available boundary or records, and hand the scope to the specialist before modeling impacts.

## Worked example & typical mistakes

A campus wants to compare single-use takeaway trays with a switched-to-reuse service that meets the same delivery and hygiene conditions. A wash operator proposes using the utility bill per day divided by meals served that day. On the scope sheet, mark two timing effects: trays may spend several days with diners, and trays from more than one outlet share each wash batch. A busy lunch day and a full wash day need not coincide. Ask the operator for load and utility records, dispatch for returns over time, and purchasing for replacements. Scenario notes can distinguish staggered returns that permit full batches from late returns that force small batches to maintain availability. Neither case has a measured environmental result.

Treating rated tray life as actual reuse counts hypothetical trips even when trays leave circulation. Keep physical durability and observed return or loss separate, and request the evidence that connects them.

## Standards, limits & handover

[ISO 14040:2006](https://www.iso.org/standard/37456.html) gives the LCA framework; [ISO 14044:2006](https://www.iso.org/standard/38498.html), §4.2, addresses goal and scope, including functional unit and system boundary. `verify` applicable amendments and requirements for the intended audience, especially a comparative assertion disclosed to the public. The JRC *ILCD Handbook: General Guide for Life Cycle Assessment — Detailed Guidance* (2010), ch. 6, offers methodological guidance on scope. The scope sheet is a working convention; actual service and operating conditions are project inputs.

Show the human the aligned boundaries, competing assumptions, and requests by owner. Operations and suppliers provide the data; the specialist determines which comparisons they support. Which service and baseline should this represent? Who records actual usage and downstream handling?
